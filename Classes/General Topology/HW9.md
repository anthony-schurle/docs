1) What are the components and path components of $\mathbb{R}_\ell$? What are the continuous maps $f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_\ell$?

Suppose $x<y$ in $\mathbb{R}_\ell$. Then $[x,y)$ is open by definition of the lower limit topology. It is also closed, since $\mathbb{R}_\ell-[x,y)=(-\infty,x)\cup [y,\infty)$, and both sets on the right are open in $\mathbb{R}_\ell$. Thus $[x,y)$ is open and closed. Now let $A\subset \mathbb{R}_\ell$ contain distinct points $x<y$. Then $A\cap [x,y)$ and $A\cap (\mathbb{R}_\ell-[x,y))$ are disjoint, nonempty, open in $A$, and their union is $A$, so they separate $A$. Hence every subset of $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ with at least two points is disconnected. Therefore the only connected subsets are singletons, so the components are the one-point sets. Since every path-connected space is connected, the path components are also singletons. If $f:\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}_\ell$ is continuous, then $f(\mathbb{R})$ is connected because $\mathbb{R}$ is connected. But the only connected subsets of $\mathbb{R}_\ell$ are singletons, so $f(\mathbb{R})$ is a singleton and $f$ is constant. Thus the continuous maps $f:\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}_\ell$ are exactly the constant maps. $\proofend$

2) Answer the following:

a) What are the components and path components of $\mathbb{R}^\omega$? (product topology)

Let $x=(x_1,x_2,\dots)$ and $y=(y_1,y_2,\dots)$ be points of $\mathbb{R}^\omega$. Define $p:[0,1]\to \mathbb{R}^\omega$ by $p(t)=((1-t)x_1+ty_1,(1-t)x_2+ty_2,\dots)$. For each $n$, $(\pi_n\circ p)(t)=(1-t)x_n+ty_n$ is continuous on $[0,1]$. Hence $p$ is continuous in the product topology. Since $p(0)=x$ and $p(1)=y$, this is a path from $x$ to $y$. Therefore $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ is path connected, hence connected. So its only component is $\mathbb{R}^\omega$, and its only path component is also $\mathbb{R}^\omega$. $\proofend$

b) Consider $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ in the uniform topology. Show that $x$ and $y$ lie in the same component of $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ iff the sequence $x-y=(x_1-y_1,x_2-y_2,\dots)$ is bounded.

By the hint, it suffices to consider the case $y=0$. Let $\bar\rho(u,v)=\sup_n \min\{|u_n-v_n|,1\}$, which induces the uniform topology on $\mathbb{R}^\omega$, and let $B=\{x\in \mathbb{R}^\omega : (x_1,x_2,\dots)\text{ is bounded}\}$. We show that $B$ is exactly the component containing $0$. First, $B$ is open. If $x\in B$, choose $M>0$ with $|x_n|\le M$ for all $n$. If $\bar\rho(x,z)<1$, then for every $n$ we have $\min\{|z_n-x_n|,1\}<1$, so $|z_n-x_n|<1$. Hence $|z_n|\le |x_n|+|z_n-x_n|<M+1$ for all $n$, so $z\in B$. Thus $B$ is open. Next, $\mathbb{R}^\omega-B$ is open. If $x\notin B$ and $\bar\rho(x,z)<1$, then again $|z_n-x_n|<1$ for all $n$. Since $x$ is unbounded, for every $N$ there is $n$ with $|x_n|>N+1$, and then $|z_n|\ge |x_n|-|z_n-x_n|>N$. So $z$ is unbounded. Hence $B$ is open and closed. Now let $x\in B$, and define $p:[0,1]\to \mathbb{R}^\omega$ by $p(t)=tx$. Choose $M>0$ with $|x_n|\le M$ for all $n$. Then for $s,t\in [0,1]$,
$\bar\rho(p(s),p(t))=\sup_n \min\{|sx_n-tx_n|,1\}=\sup_n \min\{|s-t||x_n|,1\}\le \min(M|s-t|,1)$.
Thus $p$ is continuous, and $p(0)=0$, $p(1)=x$. So every bounded sequence lies in the path component of $0$. Since $B$ is open and closed and contains $0$, every connected subset containing $0$ lies in $B$, so the component of $0$ is contained in $B$. Conversely, every point of $B$ can be joined to $0$ by a path, so $B$ lies in the component of $0$. Therefore the component of $0$ is exactly $B$. Finally, translation $T_y(z)=z-y$ is a homeomorphism of $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ in the uniform topology, so $x$ and $y$ lie in the same component iff $x-y$ and $0$ do, which holds iff $x-y$ is bounded. $\proofend$

c) Give $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ the box topology. Show that $x$ and $y$ lie in the same component of $\mathbb{R}^\omega$ iff the sequence $x-y$ is eventually zero.

Again, it suffices to consider the case $y=0$. Let $E=\{x\in \mathbb{R}^\omega : x_n=0 \text{ for all but finitely many } n\}$. We show that $E$ is exactly the component containing $0$. First let $x\in E$. Then $x_n=0$ for all $n$ outside some finite set $K$. Define $p:[0,1]\to \mathbb{R}^\omega$ by $p(t)=tx$. Let $\prod U_n$ be a basic open set containing $p(t_0)$. For each $n\in K$, continuity of $t\mapsto tx_n$ gives an open interval $W_n$ containing $t_0$ such that $tx_n\in U_n$ for all $t\in W_n$. For $n\notin K$, we have $p(t)_n=0$ for all $t$, so there is no further restriction. Since $K$ is finite, $W=\bigcap_{n\in K}W_n$ is an open neighborhood of $t_0$, and $t\in W$ implies $p(t)\in \prod U_n$. Thus $p$ is continuous. Since $p(0)=0$ and $p(1)=x$, every eventually-zero sequence lies in the path component of $0$. Now let $x\notin E$, so $x_n\neq 0$ for infinitely many $n$. Let $B=\{z\in \mathbb{R}^\omega : z \text{ is bounded}\}$. We claim that $B$ is closed and open in the box topology. If $z\in B$, choose $M$ with $|z_n|\le M$ for all $n$. Then every point of $\prod_{n=1}^\infty (z_n-1,z_n+1)$ is bounded by $M+1$, so $B$ is open. If $z\notin B$, then for every $N$ there is $n$ with $|z_n|>N+1$, and any point of $\prod_{n=1}^\infty (z_n-1,z_n+1)$ still has $n$th coordinate of absolute value $>N$; hence every point of that box is unbounded, so $\mathbb{R}^\omega-B$ is open. Thus $B$ is closed and open. For each $n$, define $h_n:\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}$ by $h_n(t)=n-\frac{n}{x_n}t$ if $x_n\neq 0$, and $h_n(t)=t$ if $x_n=0$. Each $h_n$ is a homeomorphism, so $h=\prod_{n=1}^\infty h_n:\mathbb{R}^\omega\to \mathbb{R}^\omega$ is a homeomorphism in the box topology. If $x_n\neq 0$, then $h_n(x_n)=0$, and if $x_n=0$, then also $h_n(x_n)=0$, so $h(x)=0\in B$. But for infinitely many $n$ we have $x_n\neq 0$, and then $h_n(0)=n$, so $h(0)$ is unbounded and lies outside $B$. Since $B$ is open and closed, $h(x)$ and $h(0)$ cannot lie in the same component. Because $h$ is a homeomorphism, $x$ and $0$ cannot lie in the same component either. Therefore the component of $0$ is exactly $E$. By translation, $x$ and $y$ lie in the same component iff $x-y$ is eventually zero. $\proofend$

3) a) Let $\Gamma$ and $\Gamma'$ be two topologies on the set $X$; suppose that $\Gamma\subset \Gamma'$. What does compactness of $X$ under one of these topologies imply about compactness under the other?

If $X$ is compact under $\Gamma'$, then it is compact under $\Gamma$, because every $\Gamma$-open cover is also a $\Gamma'$-open cover and hence has a finite subcover. The converse need not hold. So compactness in the finer topology implies compactness in the coarser topology, but not conversely. $\proofend$

b) Show that if $X$ is compact Hausdorff under both $\Gamma$ and $\Gamma'$, then either $\Gamma$ and $\Gamma'$ are equal or they are not comparable.

Assume $X$ is compact Hausdorff in both topologies and that they are comparable. Without loss of generality, let $\Gamma\subset \Gamma'$. Then the identity map $i:(X,\Gamma')\to (X,\Gamma)$ is a continuous bijection. Since $(X,\Gamma')$ is compact and $(X,\Gamma)$ is Hausdorff, $i$ is a homeomorphism. Hence $i^{-1}:(X,\Gamma)\to (X,\Gamma')$ is continuous, so every $\Gamma'$-open set is $\Gamma$-open. Thus $\Gamma'\subset \Gamma$, and therefore $\Gamma=\Gamma'$. So if $\Gamma\ne \Gamma'$, the two topologies are not comparable. $\proofend$

4) Show that a finite union of compact subspaces of $X$ is compact.

Let $K=\bigcup_{i=1}^n K_i$, where each $K_i$ is compact. If $\mathcal{U}$ is an open cover of $K$, then it covers each $K_i$. For each $i$, compactness of $K_i$ gives a finite subcollection $\mathcal{U}_i\subseteq \mathcal{U}$ covering $K_i$. Since there are only finitely many $i$, the union $\mathcal{U}_1\cup\cdots\cup \mathcal{U}_n$ is a finite subcover of $K$. Hence $K$ is compact. $\proofend$

5) Let $A$ and $B$ be disjoint compact subspaces of the Hausdorff space $X$. Show that there exist disjoint open sets $U$ and $V$ containing $A$ and $B$, respectively.

For each $a\in A$ and $b\in B$, choose disjoint open sets $U_{ab}$ and $V_{ab}$ with $a\in U_{ab}$ and $b\in V_{ab}$. Fix $a\in A$. Then $\{V_{ab}\}_{b\in B}$ is an open cover of $B$, so compactness of $B$ yields points $b_1,\dots,b_n\in B$ such that $B\subseteq V_{ab_1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{ab_n}$. Let $U_a=U_{ab_1}\cap\cdots\cap U_{ab_n}$ and $V_a=V_{ab_1}\cup\cdots\cup V_{ab_n}$. Then $U_a$ and $V_a$ are open, $a\in U_a$, $B\subseteq V_a$, and $U_a\cap V_a=\varnothing$. Now $\{U_a\}_{a\in A}$ covers $A$, so compactness of $A$ gives $a_1,\dots,a_m\in A$ such that $A\subseteq U_{a_1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{a_m}$. Let $U=U_{a_1}\cup\cdots\cup U_{a_m}$ and $V=V_{a_1}\cap\cdots\cap V_{a_m}$. Then $U$ and $V$ are open, $A\subseteq U$, and $B\subseteq V$. If $x\in U\cap V$, then $x\in U_{a_i}$ for some $i$ and also $x\in V_{a_i}$, contradicting $U_{a_i}\cap V_{a_i}=\varnothing$. Hence $U\cap V=\varnothing$. $\proofend$

6) Show that if $f:X\to Y$ is continuous, where $X$ is compact and $Y$ is Hausdorff, then $f$ is a closed map.

Let $C\subseteq X$ be closed. Since $X$ is compact, $C$ is compact. By continuity, $f(C)$ is compact in $Y$. Since $Y$ is Hausdorff, every compact subspace of $Y$ is closed. Thus $f(C)$ is closed in $Y$. Therefore $f$ maps closed sets to closed sets, so $f$ is a closed map. $\proofend$
