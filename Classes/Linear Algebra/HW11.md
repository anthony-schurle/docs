**LADW Chapter 9, Exercise 1.1**
(Cayley-Hamilton Theorem for diagonalizable matrices.) The Cayley-Hamilton theorem states that if $A$ is a square matrix and $p(\lambda)=\det(A-\lambda I)=\sum_{k=0}^n c_k\lambda^k$ is its characteristic polynomial, then $p(A):=\sum_{k=0}^n c_kA^k=0$, assuming $A^0=I$. Prove this theorem for the special case when $A$ is similar to a diagonal matrix, $A=SDS^{-1}$. Hint: If $D=\operatorname{diag}\{\lambda_1,\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_n\}$ and $p$ is any polynomial, can you compute $p(D)$? What about $p(A)$?
**Solution**
Suppose $A$ is diagonalizable, so $A=SDS^{-1}$, where $D=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n)$. Let $p(t)=\det(A-tI)$ be the characteristic polynomial of $A$. Since similar matrices have the same characteristic polynomial, $p(t)=\det(D-tI)$. Since $D$ is diagonal, $D-tI=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1-t,\dots,\lambda_n-t)$, so $p(t)=\prod_{j=1}^n(\lambda_j-t)$. Therefore $p(\lambda_j)=0$ for every $j=1,\dots,n$. Since powers of a diagonal matrix are computed entrywise, $p(D)=\operatorname{diag}(p(\lambda_1),\dots,p(\lambda_n))$. But each $p(\lambda_j)=0$, so $p(D)=0$. Now write $p(t)=c_0+c_1t+\cdots+c_nt^n$. Since the book has already shown that powers satisfy $A^k=SD^kS^{-1}$, we get:
$$  
\begin{aligned}  
p(A)  
&=c_0I+c_1A+\cdots+c_nA^n\\  
&=c_0SIS^{-1}+c_1SDS^{-1}+\cdots+c_nSD^nS^{-1}\\  
&=S(c_0I+c_1D+\cdots+c_nD^n)S^{-1}\\  
&=Sp(D)S^{-1}.  
\end{aligned}  
$$

Since $p(D) = 0$, it follows that $p(A)=S0S^{-1}=0$. Hence the Cayley-Hamilton theorem holds for diagonalizable matrices. $\proofend$

**LADW Chapter 9, Exercise 2.1**
An operator $A$ is called nilpotent if $A^k=0$ for some $k$. Prove that if $A$ is nilpotent, then $\sigma(A)=\{0\}$, that is, $0$ is the only eigenvalue of $A$. Can you do it without using the spectral mapping theorem?
**Solution**
Suppose $A$ is nilpotent. Then there exists a positive integer $k$ such that $A^k=0$. First, we show that $0$ is an eigenvalue of $A$. From $A^k=0$, we get $\det(A^k)=\det(0)=0$. Since determinant is multiplicative for square matrices (A is square by definition of a defined $\sigma$), $\det(A^k)=\det(A)^k$. Hence $\det(A)^k=0$, so $\det(A)=0$. Therefore $A$ is not invertible, so $\ker A\neq\{0\}$. Thus there exists a nonzero vector $x$ such that $Ax=0=0x$, which means $0$ is an eigenvalue of $A$. Now let $\lambda\in\sigma(A)$. Then there exists a nonzero vector $x$ such that $Ax=\lambda x$. Applying $A^k$ to $x$, we get $A^kx=\lambda^kx$. But $A^k=0$, so $0=\lambda^kx$. Since $x\neq 0$, it follows that $\lambda^k=0$, and therefore $\lambda=0$. Hence every eigenvalue of $A$ is $0$. Since $0$ is an eigenvalue and no other eigenvalue is possible, $\sigma(A)=\{0\}$. $\proofend$

**Additional Problem 1**
Let $V$ be a vector space over the field $\mathbb{F}$, which is either the reals or the complex numbers. Let $A$ be a linear transformation $A:V\to V$. Let $p$ be a polynomial and consider the subspace $W=\ker(p(A))$, the kernel of the linear transformation $p(A)$. Show that $W$ is invariant under $A$.
**Solution**
Take $w\in W$. Then $p(A)w=0$. Since $p(A)$ is a polynomial in $A$, it commutes with $A$, so $p(A)A=Ap(A)$. Therefore $p(A)(Aw)=A(p(A)w)=A0=0$. Hence $Aw\in\ker p(A)=W$. Therefore $W$ is invariant under $A$. $\proofend$

**Additional Problem 2**
Let $V$ be a vector space over $\mathbb{F}$ and let $A$ be a linear transformation from $V$ to $V$ and $E$ a linear transformation from $V$ to $V$ with $E^2=E$. Show that the range of $E$ is invariant under $A$ if and only if $EAE=AE$.
**Solution**
First suppose $\operatorname{Ran}E$ is invariant under $A$. Take any $v\in V$. Then $Ev\in\operatorname{Ran}E$, so by invariance, $AEv\in\operatorname{Ran}E$. Since $AEv\in\operatorname{Ran}E$, there exists some $x\in V$ such that $AEv=Ex$. Then $E(AEv)=E(Ex)=E^2x=Ex=AEv$. Therefore $EAEv=AEv$ for every $v\in V$, so $EAE=AE$. Conversely, suppose $EAE=AE$. Let $y\in\operatorname{Ran}E$. Then $y=Ev$ for some $v\in V$. Hence $Ay=A(Ev)=AEv$. Using $EAE=AE$, we get $AEv=EAEv$. Thus $Ay=EAEv$, which is in $\operatorname{Ran}E$. Therefore $\operatorname{Ran}E$ is invariant under $A$. Hence $\operatorname{Ran}E$ is invariant under $A$ if and only if $EAE=AE$. $\proofend$

**Additional Problem 3**
If $V$ is a vector space and $W_1$ and $W_2$ are subspaces, we say that $V=W_1\oplus W_2$ if every vector $\vec{x}$ in $V$ can be written uniquely as a sum of a vector in $W_1$ and a vector in $W_2$. Let $T$ be an invertible linear transformation on $V$, a finite dimensional vector space. Suppose $V=W_1\oplus W_2$. Show that $V=T(W_1)\oplus T(W_2)$. Here $T(W_1)$ and $T(W_2)$ denote respectively the range of $T$ when viewed as a linear transformation from $W_1$ to $V$ and the range of $T$ when viewed as a linear transformation from $W_2$ to $V$.
**Solution**
First show that $V=T(W_1)+T(W_2)$. Take any $v\in V$. Since $T$ is invertible, it is surjective, so there exists $x\in V$ such that $v=Tx$. Since $V=W_1\oplus W_2$, there exist unique $w_1\in W_1$ and $w_2\in W_2$ such that $x=w_1+w_2$. Therefore $v=Tx=T(w_1+w_2)=Tw_1+Tw_2$, where $Tw_1\in T(W_1)$ and $Tw_2\in T(W_2)$. Hence $V=T(W_1)+T(W_2)$. Suppose $y\in T(W_1)\cap T(W_2)$. Then $y=Tw_1=Tw_2$ for some $w_1\in W_1$ and $w_2\in W_2$. Since $T$ is invertible, it is injective, so $w_1=w_2$. Thus $w_1=w_2\in W_1\cap W_2$. Because $V=W_1\oplus W_2$, we have $W_1\cap W_2=\{0\}$. Therefore $w_1=w_2=0$, so $y=0$. Hence $T(W_1)\cap T(W_2)=\{0\}$. Since $V=T(W_1)+T(W_2)$ and $T(W_1)\cap T(W_2)=\{0\}$, we conclude that $V=T(W_1)\oplus T(W_2)$. $\proofend$

**Additional Problem 4**
With notation the same as in Problem 3, let $V$ be a finite dimensional vector space. Let $T$ be a linear transformation from $V$ to $V$ with the property that for every two subspaces of $V$ with $V=W_1\oplus W_2$, we have $V=T(W_1)\oplus T(W_2)$. Show that $T$ is invertible.
**Solution**
Consider the particular direct sum decomposition $V=V\oplus\{0\}$. By the hypothesis, $V=T(V)\oplus T(\{0\})$. Since $T$ is linear, $T(\{0\})=\{0\}$. Therefore $V=T(V)\oplus\{0\}$, which implies $V=T(V)$. So $T$ is surjective. By rank-nullity, $\dim V=\dim\operatorname{Ran}T+\dim\ker T$. Since $T$ is surjective, $\operatorname{Ran}T=V$, so $\dim\operatorname{Ran}T=\dim V$. Thus $\dim V=\dim V+\dim\ker T$, which implies $\dim\ker T=0$. Hence $\ker T=\{0\}$, so $T$ is injective. Therefore $T$ is invertible. $\proofend$

**Additional Problem 5**
We say that a matrix $N$ is nilpotent if there is some positive integer $k$ so that $N^k$ is the zero matrix. We call $d$ the minimal power for $N$ if $d$ is the smallest positive integer so that $N^d$ is the zero matrix. Let $N_1$ and $N_2$ be $6\times 6$ matrices which are nilpotent. Suppose that $N_1$ and $N_2$ have the same nullity, that is, the same dimension of kernel, and the same minimal power. Show that then $N_1$ and $N_2$ are similar. Give an example which shows that the same is not true for $7\times 7$ matrices.
**Solution**
By the Jordan canonical form theorem for nilpotent matrices, every nilpotent matrix is similar to a block diagonal matrix whose blocks are nilpotent Jordan blocks $J_m(0)$. For a nilpotent Jordan block $J_m(0)$, we have $\dim\ker J_m(0)=1$. Also, $J_m(0)^m=0$ but $J_m(0)^{m-1}\neq 0$. Thus, if the Jordan block sizes of $N$ are $\lambda_1\geq\lambda_2\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_r$, then $\dim\ker N=r$, because each Jordan block contributes one dimension to the kernel. Also, the minimal power of $N$ is $\lambda_1$, the size of the largest Jordan block. Now we list the partitions of $6$, which correspond to the possible Jordan block sizes for a $6\times 6$ nilpotent matrix:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
\text{Jordan block sizes} & \dim\ker N & \text{minimal power} \\
\hline
6 & 1 & 6\\
5+1 & 2 & 5\\
4+2 & 2 & 4\\
4+1+1 & 3 & 4\\
3+3 & 2 & 3\\
3+2+1 & 3 & 3\\
3+1+1+1 & 4 & 3\\
2+2+2 & 3 & 2\\
2+2+1+1 & 4 & 2\\
2+1+1+1+1 & 5 & 2\\
1+1+1+1+1+1 & 6 & 1
\end{array}
$$
In this table, each ordered pair $(\dim\ker N,\text{ minimal power})$ appears exactly once. Therefore, for $6\times 6$ nilpotent matrices, the nullity and minimal power determine the Jordan block sizes uniquely. Since two nilpotent matrices are similar if and only if they have the same Jordan block sizes, it follows that $N_1$ and $N_2$ are similar. Now we give an example showing that this is not true for $7\times 7$ matrices. Let
$$
N_1=\operatorname{diag}(J_3(0),J_3(0),J_1(0))
$$
and
$$
N_2=\operatorname{diag}(J_3(0),J_2(0),J_2(0)).
$$
Both $N_1$ and $N_2$ are $7\times 7$ nilpotent matrices. The Jordan block sizes of $N_1$ are $3+3+1$, while the Jordan block sizes of $N_2$ are $3+2+2$. Both matrices have three Jordan blocks, so $\dim\ker N_1=\dim\ker N_2=3$. Both matrices also have largest Jordan block size $3$, so both have minimal power $3$. Thus $N_1$ and $N_2$ have the same nullity and the same minimal power. However, they are not similar, because their Jordan block sizes are different: $3+3+1\neq 3+2+2$. Equivalently, their squared ranks are different. Since $\operatorname{rank}(J_3(0)^2)=1$, $\operatorname{rank}(J_2(0)^2)=0$, and $\operatorname{rank}(J_1(0)^2)=0$, we get
$$
\operatorname{rank}(N_1^2)=1+1+0=2
$$
but
$$
\operatorname{rank}(N_2^2)=1+0+0=1.
$$
If $N_1$ and $N_2$ were similar, then $N_1^2$ and $N_2^2$ would also be similar, so they would have the same rank. Since their squared ranks are different, $N_1$ and $N_2$ are not similar. This example shows that the corresponding statement is false for $7\times 7$ nilpotent matrices. $\proofend$
