**Problem 1.1**  
Find the matrix of the bilinear form $L$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$, $L(x, y) = x_1y_1 + 2x_1 y_2 + 14x_1 y_3 - 5x_2 y_1 + 2x_2 y_2 - 3x_2 y_3 + 8x_3 y_1 + 19x_3 y_2 - 2x_3y_3$.
**Solution**  
By definition, $L(x, y) = \sum_{j, k = 1}^n a_{j,k}x_k y_j = (Ax, y)$, where $a_{j,k}$ is an entry of the matrix $A$ for $L$. Since $L(x, y) = x_1y_1 + 2x_1 y_2 + 14x_1 y_3 - 5x_2 y_1 + 2x_2 y_2 - 3x_2 y_3 + 8x_3 y_1 + 19x_3 y_2 - 2x_3y_3$, the corresponding matrix must be
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & -5 & 8 \\
2 & 2 & 19 \\
14 & -3 & -2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
$\proofend$

**Problem 1.2**  
Define the bilinear form $L$ on $\mathbb{R}^2$ by $L(x, y) = \det[x, y]$. Find the matrix of $L$.
**Solution**  
Let $x_1, x_2$ denote the first and second coordinates of $x$ respectively, and similarly for $y_1, y_2$ on $y$. Then $\det[x, y] = x_1y_2 - y_1x_2 = x_1y_2 - x_2y_1$. By definition, $L(x, y) = \sum_{j, k = 1}^n a_{j,k}x_k y_j = (Ax, y)$, where $a_{j,k}$ is an entry of the matrix $A$ for $L$. Clearly, $A$ is then defined by
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & -1 \\
1 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
$\proofend$

**Problem 1.3**  
Find the matrix of the quadratic form $Q$ on $\mathbb{R}^3$, $Q[x] = x_1^2 + 2x_1x_2 - 3x_1x_3 - 9x_2^2 + 6x_2x_3 + 13x_3^2$.
**Solution**  
By definition, $Q[x] = L(x, x) = (Ax, x) = \sum_{j, k = 1}^n a_{j, k} x_kx_j$. Since the matrix of a quadratic form is symmetric, we split the mixed terms evenly. Thus, the matrix $A$ must be
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 & -\frac{3}{2} \\
1 & -9 & 3 \\
-\frac{3}{2} & 3 & 13
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
$\proofend$

**Problem 1.4**  
Prove Lemma 1.1.
**Solution**  
Suppose $(Ax,x)$ is real for all $x\in\mathbb{C}^n$. We show that $A$ is Hermitian. Fix $x,y\in\mathbb{C}^n$ and define $q(z)=(A(x+zy),x+zy)$, where $z\in\mathbb{C}$. Expanding, we get $q(z)=(Ax,x)+\overline{z}(Ax,y)+z(Ay,x)+|z|^2(Ay,y)$. Since $(Au,u)$ is real for all $u\in\mathbb{C}^n$, $q(z)$ must be real for every $z\in\mathbb{C}$. Also, $(Ax,x)$ and $|z|^2(Ay,y)$ are real, so it follows that $\overline{z}(Ax,y)+z(Ay,x)$ is real for all $z\in\mathbb{C}$. Taking $z=1$, we get $(Ax,y)+(Ay,x)\in\mathbb{R}$. Taking $z=i$, we get $-i(Ax,y)+i(Ay,x)\in\mathbb{R}$. Let $(Ax,y)=a+bi$ and $(Ay,x)=c+di$, where $a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}$. Then the first condition implies $b+d=0$, and the second implies $c-a=0$. Hence $c=a$ and $d=-b$, so $(Ay,x)=a-bi=\overline{a+bi}=\overline{(Ax,y)}$. Therefore, $(Ax,y)=\overline{(Ay,x)}$. By the adjoint property, $(Ay,x)=(y,A^*x)$, so $(Ax,y)=\overline{(y,A^*x)}=(A^*x,y)$. Thus $(Ax,y)=(A^*x,y)$ for all $y\in\mathbb{C}^n$. Hence $Ax=A^*x$ for all $x\in\mathbb{C}^n$, and therefore $A=A^*$. So $A$ is Hermitian.  
$\proofend$

**Problem 2.1**  
Diagonalize the quadratic form with the matrix
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 2 & 1 \\
2 & 3 & 2 \\
1 & 2 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Use two methods: completion of squares and row operations. Which one do you like better? Can you say if the matrix $A$ is positive definite or not?
**Solution**  
*Completion Of Squares -* We see that the quadratic form for matrix $A$ is $Q[x] = x_1^2 + 3x_2^2 + x_3^2 + 4x_1x_2 + 2x_1x_3 + 4x_2x_3$. Since
$$
(x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3)^2 = x_1^2 + 4x_2^2 + x_3^2 + 4x_1x_2 + 2x_1x_3 + 4x_2x_3,
$$
we can rewrite $Q[x]$ as $Q[x] = (x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3)^2 - x_2^2$. So, setting $y_1 = x_1 + 2x_2 + x_3$, $y_2 = x_2$, and $y_3 = x_3$, we get $Q[x] = y_1^2 - y_2^2$. Thus, the diagonal form is $y_1^2 - y_2^2 + 0y_3^2$, so the corresponding diagonal matrix is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & -1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
*Row Operations -* Since this is a quadratic form, we use simultaneous row and column operations. Starting with
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix}
1 & 2 & 1 \\
2 & 3 & 2 \\
1 & 2 & 1
\end{pmatrix},
$$
apply $R_2 \to R_2 - 2R_1$ and $C_2 \to C_2 - 2C_1$. This gives
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 1 \\
0 & -1 & 0 \\
1 & 0 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Now apply $R_3 \to R_3 - R_1$ and $C_3 \to C_3 - C_1$. This gives
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & -1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
So again the diagonal form is
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & -1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & 0
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
I liked completion of squares more because writing matrices in LaTeX is tedious. Also, $A$ is not positive definite. For instance, let $(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (-2, 1, 0)$. Then $Q[x] = (-2)^2 + 3(1)^2 + 0 + 4(-2)(1) + 0 + 0 = -1 < 0$, so $A$ cannot be positive definite.  
$\proofend$

**Problem 2.2**  
For the matrix $A$,
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix}
2 & 1 & 1 \\
1 & 2 & 1 \\
1 & 1 & 2
\end{pmatrix},
$$
orthogonally diagonalize the corresponding quadratic form.
**Solution** 
Consider the vectors $u_1 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}(1,1,1)$, $u_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(1,-1,0)$, and $u_3 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}}(1,1,-2)$. These vectors are orthonormal. Also,
$$
A\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}4\\4\\4\end{pmatrix}
=
4\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
A\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
So $u_1$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $4$, while $u_2$ and $u_3$ are linearly independent eigenvectors with eigenvalue $1$. Let $Q$ be the orthogonal matrix whose columns are $u_1,u_2,u_3$:
$$
Q =
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} & -\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} & \frac{1}{\sqrt{6}} \\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} & 0 & -\frac{2}{\sqrt{6}}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Then $Q^T A Q = \operatorname{diag}(4,1,1)$. Therefore, the corresponding quadratic form is orthogonally diagonalized as $Q[x] = 4y_1^2 + y_2^2 + y_3^2$.
$\proofend$

**Problem 4.1**  
Using Sylvester's Criterion of Positivity check if the matrices,
$$
A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad B = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -1 & 2 \\ -1 & 4 & -2 \\ 2 & -2 & 1 \end{pmatrix}
$$
are positive definite or not. Are the matrices $-A, A^3,$ and $A^{-1}, A + B^{-1}, A + B, A - B$ positive definite?
**Solution**  
By Sylvester's Criterion, a real symmetric matrix is positive definite if and only if the determinants of all upper left submatrices are positive.

For $A$, we compute $\det A_1 = 4 > 0$, and
$$
\det A_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{vmatrix} = 4\cdot 3 - 2\cdot 2 = 12 - 4 = 8 > 0.
$$
For the full determinant,
$$
\det A = \begin{vmatrix} 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{vmatrix}.
$$
Expanding along the first row,
$$
\det A = 4\begin{vmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} - 2\begin{vmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} + \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix}.
$$
Now $\begin{vmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 3\cdot 2 - (-1)(-1) = 6 - 1 = 5$, $\begin{vmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ 1 & 2 \end{vmatrix} = 2\cdot 2 - (-1)(1) = 4 + 1 = 5$, and $\begin{vmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = 2(-1) - 3\cdot 1 = -2 - 3 = -5$. So $\det A = 4(5) - 2(5) + (-5) = 20 - 10 - 5 = 5 > 0$. Since all three determinants are positive, $A$ is positive definite.

For $B$, we compute $\det B_1 = 3 > 0$, and
$$
\det B_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 3 & -1 \\ -1 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = 3\cdot 4 - (-1)(-1) = 12 - 1 = 11 > 0.
$$
For the full determinant,
$$
\det B = \begin{vmatrix} 3 & -1 & 2 \\ -1 & 4 & -2 \\ 2 & -2 & 1 \end{vmatrix}.
$$
Expanding along the first row,
$$
\det B = 3\begin{vmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} - (-1)\begin{vmatrix} -1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} + 2\begin{vmatrix} -1 & 4 \\ 2 & -2 \end{vmatrix}.
$$
Now $\begin{vmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = 4\cdot 1 - (-2)(-2) = 4 - 4 = 0$, $\begin{vmatrix} -1 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{vmatrix} = (-1)(1) - (-2)(2) = -1 + 4 = 3$, and $\begin{vmatrix} -1 & 4 \\ 2 & -2 \end{vmatrix} = (-1)(-2) - 4\cdot 2 = 2 - 8 = -6$. So $\det B = 3(0) + 1(3) + 2(-6) = 3 - 12 = -9 < 0$. Therefore $B$ is not positive definite.

Now for the other matrices. First, $-A$ is not positive definite, since $A$ is positive definite and so $-A$ is negative definite (By Sylvester's Criterion as noted in the book).

Next, compute
$$
A^2 = A\cdot A = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 21 & 13 & 4 \\ 13 & 14 & -3 \\ 4 & -3 & 6 \end{pmatrix},
$$
and then
$$
A^3 = A^2A = \begin{pmatrix} 21 & 13 & 4 \\ 13 & 14 & -3 \\ 4 & -3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & -1 \\ 1 & -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 114 & 77 & 16 \\ 77 & 71 & -7 \\ 16 & -7 & 19 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
Now check Sylvester's Criterion for $A^3$: $\det (A^3)_1 = 114 > 0$, and
$$
\det (A^3)_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 114 & 77 \\ 77 & 71 \end{vmatrix} = 114\cdot 71 - 77^2 = 8094 - 5929 = 2165 > 0.
$$
Also, $\det(A^3) = (\det A)^3 = 5^3 = 125 > 0$. Hence $A^3$ is positive definite.

Also,
$$
A^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & -1 & -1 \\ -1 & \frac75 & \frac65 \\ -1 & \frac65 & \frac85 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its left upper submatrices are $\det(A^{-1})_1 = 1 > 0$ and
$$
\det(A^{-1})_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & -1 \\ -1 & \frac75 \end{vmatrix} = \frac75 - 1 = \frac25 > 0.
$$
Also, $\det(A^{-1}) = \frac{1}{\det A} = \frac15 > 0$. So $A^{-1}$ is positive definite.

Next,
$$
B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & \frac13 & \frac23 \\ \frac13 & \frac19 & -\frac49 \\ \frac23 & -\frac49 & -\frac{11}{9} \end{pmatrix},
$$
so
$$
A + B^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix} 4 & \frac73 & \frac53 \\ \frac73 & \frac{28}{9} & -\frac{13}{9} \\ \frac53 & -\frac{13}{9} & \frac79 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its upper left submatrices are $\det (A + B^{-1})_1 = 4 > 0$, and
$$
\det (A + B^{-1})_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 4 & \frac73 \\ \frac73 & \frac{28}{9} \end{vmatrix} = 4\cdot \frac{28}{9} - \frac73\cdot \frac73 = \frac{112}{9} - \frac{49}{9} = \frac{63}{9} = 7 > 0.
$$
For the full determinant,
$$
\det(A+B^{-1}) = \begin{vmatrix} 4 & \frac73 & \frac53 \\ \frac73 & \frac{28}{9} & -\frac{13}{9} \\ \frac53 & -\frac{13}{9} & \frac79 \end{vmatrix} = -\frac{205}{9} < 0.
$$
So $A + B^{-1}$ is not positive definite.

Also,
$$
A + B = \begin{pmatrix} 7 & 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 7 & -3 \\ 3 & -3 & 3 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its upper left submatrices are $\det (A+B)_1 = 7 > 0$, and
$$
\det (A+B)_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 7 & 1 \\ 1 & 7 \end{vmatrix} = 49 - 1 = 48 > 0.
$$
For the full determinant,
$$
\det(A+B) = \begin{vmatrix} 7 & 1 & 3 \\ 1 & 7 & -3 \\ 3 & -3 & 3 \end{vmatrix}.
$$
Expanding along the first row,
$$
\det(A+B) = 7\begin{vmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -3 & 3 \end{vmatrix} - \begin{vmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ 3 & 3 \end{vmatrix} + 3\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 7 \\ 3 & -3 \end{vmatrix}.
$$
Now $\begin{vmatrix} 7 & -3 \\ -3 & 3 \end{vmatrix} = 7\cdot 3 - (-3)(-3) = 21 - 9 = 12$, $\begin{vmatrix} 1 & -3 \\ 3 & 3 \end{vmatrix} = 1\cdot 3 - (-3)(3) = 3 + 9 = 12$, and $\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 7 \\ 3 & -3 \end{vmatrix} = 1(-3) - 7\cdot 3 = -3 - 21 = -24$. So $\det(A+B)=7(12)-12+3(-24)=84-12-72=0$. Hence $A+B$ is not positive definite.

Finally,
$$
A - B = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 3 & -1 \\ 3 & -1 & 1 \\ -1 & 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its upper left submatrices are $\det (A-B)_1 = 1 > 0$, and
$$
\det (A-B)_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 3 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = 1(-1) - 3\cdot 3 = -1 - 9 = -10 < 0.
$$
Since the second submatrix is negative, $A-B$ is not positive definite.

Therefore: $A$ is positive definite; $B$ is not positive definite; $-A$ is not positive definite; $A^3$ is positive definite; $A^{-1}$ is positive definite; $A+B^{-1}$ is not positive definite; $A+B$ is not positive definite; and $A-B$ is not positive definite.
$\proofend$

**Problem 4.2**  
True or False: a) If $A$ is positive definite, then $A^5$ is positive definite. b) If $A$ is negative definite, then $A^8$ is negative definite. c) If $A$ is negative definite, then $A^{12}$ is positive definite. d) If $A$ is positive definite and $B$ is negative semidefinite, then $A - B$ is positive definite. e) If $A$ is indefinite, and $B$ is positive definite, then $A + B$ is indefinite.
**Solution**  
Theorem 4.1 applies throughout. Since $A$ is Hermitian, it has an orthonormal basis of eigenvectors. If $Av=\lambda v$, then $A^m v=\lambda^m v$. Therefore the same eigenbasis diagonalizes $A^m$, so the eigenvalues of $A^m$ are exactly the $m$-th powers of the eigenvalues of $A$, with no new eigenvalues appearing.

a) True. Since $A$ is positive definite, Theorem 4.1 implies that all eigenvalues of $A$ are positive. Therefore the eigenvalues of $A^5$ are $\lambda^5>0$, so by Theorem 4.1, $A^5$ is positive definite.

b) False. Since $A$ is negative definite, Theorem 4.1 implies that all eigenvalues of $A$ are negative. Therefore the eigenvalues of $A^8$ are $\lambda^8>0$, so by Theorem 4.1, $A^8$ is positive definite, not negative definite.

c) True. Since $A$ is negative definite, Theorem 4.1 implies that all eigenvalues of $A$ are negative. Therefore the eigenvalues of $A^{12}$ are $\lambda^{12}>0$, so by Theorem 4.1, $A^{12}$ is positive definite.

d) True. For every $x\ne 0$, $((A-B)x,x)=(Ax,x)-(Bx,x)$. Since $A$ is positive definite, $(Ax,x)>0$, and since $B$ is negative semidefinite, $(Bx,x)\le 0$. Hence $((A-B)x,x)>0$ for all $x\ne 0$. Also, $A-B$ is Hermitian since $(A-B)^*=A^*-B^*=A-B$. Therefore $A-B$ is positive definite.

e) False. Consider
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Then $A$ is indefinite and $B$ is positive definite, but
$$
A+B=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&3\end{pmatrix},
$$
whose eigenvalues are $1$ and $3$. Therefore, by Theorem 4.1, $A+B$ is positive definite, not indefinite.
$\proofend$

**Problem 4.4**  
Find a real symmetric $n \times n$ matrix $A$ such that $\det A_k \ge 0$ for all $k = 1, 2, ..., n$, but the matrix $A$ is not positive semidefinite.  
**Solution**  
Take $n = 3$ and let  
$$  
A = \begin{pmatrix}  
1 & 0 & 0 \\  
0 & 0 & 0 \\  
0 & 0 & -1  
\end{pmatrix}.  
$$  
This matrix is real symmetric. Its upper left submatrices are $\det A_1 = 1$, $\det A_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{vmatrix} = 0$, and $\det A_3 = \det A = 0$. So $\det A_k \ge 0$ for all $k = 1,2,3$. However, $A$ is not positive semidefinite, since for $x = (0,0,1)^T$ we have $(Ax, x) = -1 < 0$.  
$\proofend$
