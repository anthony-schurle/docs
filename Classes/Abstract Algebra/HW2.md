## Problem 1

Level A (10 points). These are the basic properties of groups stated in class. Let $G$ be a group and let $a,b,c\in G$. Using only the group axioms, prove each of the following statements.

1. The identity element of $G$ is unique. That is, if $e$ and $f$ both satisfy $ex=xe=x$ and $fx=xf=x$ for every $x\in G$, then $e=f$.
2. The inverse of $a$ is unique. That is, if $ab=ba=e$ and $ac=ca=e$, then $b=c$.
3. $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}$.
4. $(a^{-1})^{-1}=a$.
5. Both cancellation laws hold: if $ab=ac$ then $b=c$, and if $ba=ca$ then $b=c$.

Proofs:

1. Since $f$ is an identity, $xf=x$ for every $x\in G$. Taking $x=e$ gives $ef=e$. Likewise, since $e$ is an identity, $ex=x$ for every $x\in G$. Taking $x=f$ gives $ef=f$. Therefore $e=ef=f$, proving that the identity is unique. $\proofend$
2. Suppose $ab=ba=e$ and $ac=ca=e$. The identity axiom states that $xe=ex=x$ for every $x\in G$, so $be=b$ and $ec=c$. Using $ac=e$, associativity, and $ba=e$, we obtain $b=be=b(ac)=(ba)c=ec=c$. Therefore any two inverses of $a$ are equal. $\proofend$
3. By the inverse axiom, $bb^{-1}=b^{-1}b=e$ and $aa^{-1}=a^{-1}a=e$. Using associativity to regroup factors and the identity equations $xe=ex=x$ for every $x\in G$, we obtain $(ab)(b^{-1}a^{-1})=a(bb^{-1})a^{-1}=(ae)a^{-1}=aa^{-1}=e$. Similarly, $(b^{-1}a^{-1})(ab)=b^{-1}(a^{-1}a)b=(b^{-1}e)b=b^{-1}b=e$. Thus $b^{-1}a^{-1}$ satisfies both equations defining an inverse of $ab$. By uniqueness of inverses from Part 2, $(ab)^{-1}=b^{-1}a^{-1}$. $\proofend$
4. An inverse of $a^{-1}$ is an element $x\in G$ satisfying $a^{-1}x=xa^{-1}=e$. By the inverse axiom applied to $a$, we have $a^{-1}a=aa^{-1}=e$, so $x=a$ satisfies both equations. By uniqueness of inverses from Part 2, $(a^{-1})^{-1}=a$. $\proofend$
5. If $ab=ac$, multiplying both sides on the left by $a^{-1}$ gives $a^{-1}(ab)=a^{-1}(ac)$. Associativity gives $(a^{-1}a)b=(a^{-1}a)c$, and the inverse axiom gives $eb=ec$. Since $ex=x$ for every $x\in G$, we conclude $b=c$. Similarly, if $ba=ca$, multiplying both sides on the right by $a^{-1}$ gives $(ba)a^{-1}=(ca)a^{-1}$. Associativity gives $b(aa^{-1})=c(aa^{-1})$, and the inverse axiom gives $be=ce$. Since $xe=x$ for every $x\in G$, we again conclude $b=c$. Thus both cancellation laws hold. $\proofend$

## Problem 3

Level B (20 points).

1. Let $H$ be a finite nonempty subset of a group $G$. Prove that $H$ is a subgroup of $G$ if and only if $xy\in H$ for all $x,y\in H$. Is this still true if $H$ is allowed to be infinite? Prove your answer.
2. Let $G$ be a finite group of order $n>2$. Prove that $G$ has no subgroup of order $n-1$. You may not use Lagrange’s theorem, which we prove later in the course.

Proofs:

1. 1. $(\Rightarrow)$ Assume $H$ is a subgroup of $G$. Then $H$ is itself a group under the operation inherited from $G$, so it is closed under that operation. Therefore $xy\in H$ for all $x,y\in H$. $(\Leftarrow)$ Conversely, assume $H$ is a finite nonempty subset of $G$ satisfying $xy\in H$ for all $x,y\in H$. We must prove that $H$ is a subgroup. Choose any $h\in H$. Closure implies that every positive power of $h$ belongs to $H$. Since $H$ is finite, $h^i=h^j$ for some positive integers $i<j$. Writing $h^ie=h^ih^{j-i}$ and cancelling in $G$ gives $e=h^{j-i}$. Let $r=j-i>0$. Then $e=h^r\in H$, and $hh^{r-1}=h^{r-1}h=e$, so $h^{-1}=h^{r-1}$. If $r>1$, this belongs to $H$ by closure; if $r=1$, it equals $e$, which is already in $H$. Since $h$ was arbitrary, $H$ contains every element’s inverse. Thus $H$ contains the identity and is closed under multiplication and inverses, so Proposition 32 gives $H\le G$. The equivalence fails for infinite subsets: $H=\{0,1,2,\ldots\}$ is a nonempty subset of $(\mathbb Z,+)$ closed under addition, but it is not a subgroup because $1\in H$ while $-1\notin H$. $\proofend$
2. Suppose, toward a contradiction, that $H\le G$ has order $n-1$. There is exactly one element $a\in G\setminus H$. Since $n>2$, the subgroup $H$ contains an element $h\ne e$. We cannot have $ah\in H$, because then $a=(ah)h^{-1}\in H$. Therefore $ah\notin H$. Since $a$ is the only element outside $H$, we have $ah=a=ae$. Cancellation gives $h=e$, a contradiction. Hence no subgroup has order $n-1$. $\proofend$

## Problem 4

Level B (20 points). Consider the additive group $(\mathbb Q,+)$ of rational numbers.

1. Prove that every finitely generated subgroup of $(\mathbb Q,+)$ is cyclic. Here a subgroup $H$ is finitely generated if there are finitely many elements $q_1,\ldots,q_r\in\mathbb Q$ with $H=\langle q_1,\ldots,q_r\rangle$.
2. Prove that $(\mathbb Q,+)$ is not finitely generated.

You may use without proof that every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic.

Proofs:

1. The trivial subgroup is cyclic, generated by $0$. Otherwise, write $H=\langle q_1,\ldots,q_r\rangle$, where $q_i=a_i/b_i$ and $b_i>0$. Choose a positive common denominator $D$, for example $D=b_1\cdots b_r$. Each $q_i$ is an integer multiple of $1/D$. Hence the cyclic subgroup (Theorem 34) $K=\langle 1/D\rangle=\{k/D:k\in\mathbb Z\}$ contains every generator of $H$, so $H \le K$. By Theorem 36, every subgroup of a cyclic group is cyclic. Therefore $H$ is cyclic. $\proofend$
2. If $\mathbb Q$ had a finite generating set, the same common-denominator argument would give $\mathbb Q\subseteq\{k/D:k\in\mathbb Z\}$ for some positive integer $D$. But $1/(2D)\notin\{k/D:k\in\mathbb Z\}$, since $1/(2D)=k/D$ would require $k=1/2$. Thus $(\mathbb Q,+)$ is not finitely generated. $\proofend$
