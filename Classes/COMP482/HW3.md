**Problem (2a, 2b, 2c, 2d)**
Write a regular expressions to describe each of the following languages:
a) $\{w \in \{a, b\}^* | \text{every a in w is immediately preceded and followed by b}\}$.
b) $\{w \in \{a, b\}^* | \text{w does not end in ba}\}$.
c) $\{w \in \{0, 1\}^* | \exists y \in \{0, 1\}^* (|wy| \text{ is even})\}$.
d) 
$$
\left\{
w \in \{0,1\}^*
\;\middle|\;
\substack{
w \text{ is the binary encoding, without leading 0's,}\\
\text{of a natural number evenly divisible by }4
}
\right\}
$$
**Solution**
a)
$\epsilon \cup b(b \cup ab)^*$.

b)
$(a \cup b)^*(aa \cup ab \cup bb) \cup a \cup b \cup \epsilon$.

c)
$(0 \cup 1)^*$.

d)
$0 \cup 1(0 \cup 1)^*00$.

**Problem (7a, 7b)**
Use the algorithm presented in the proof of Kleene’s Theorem to construct an FSM to accept the language generated by each of the following regular expressions:
a) $(b(b \cup \epsilon)b)^*$.
b) $bab \cup a^*$.
**Solution**
a)
We begin with the primitive subexpressions $b$ and $\epsilon$. For $b$ we have $M_b = (\{b_1, b_2\}, \Sigma, \{((b_1, b), b_2)\}, b_1, \{b_2\})$ and for $\epsilon$ we have $M_\epsilon = (\{\epsilon_1\}, \Sigma, \emptyset, \epsilon_1, \{\epsilon_1\})$. Now we construct the machine for $b \cup \epsilon$. Since $\{\epsilon_1\} \cap \{b_1, b_2\} = \emptyset$, no state relabeling is needed. The machine constructed is $M_{b \cup \epsilon} = (\{s, \epsilon_1, b_1, b_2\}, \Sigma, \Delta_{b \cup \epsilon}, s, \{b_2, \epsilon_1\})$ where $\Delta_{b \cup \epsilon} = \{((b_1, b), b_2), ((s, \epsilon), \epsilon_1), ((s, \epsilon), b_1)\}$. Now the FSM for the expression $b(b \cup \epsilon)$ becomes (after relabeling $M_b$ states to $b_1 = b_1'$ and $b_2 = b_2'$) $M_{b(b \cup \epsilon)} = (\{b_1', b_2', s, \epsilon_1, b_1, b_2\}, \Sigma, \Delta_{b(b \cup \epsilon)}, b_1', \{b_2, \epsilon_1\})$ where $\Delta_{b(b \cup \epsilon)} = \{((b_2', \epsilon), s), ((b_1, b), b_2), ((s, \epsilon), \epsilon_1), ((s, \epsilon), b_1), ((b_1', b), b_2')\}$. Next we construct $b(b \cup \epsilon)b$ (after relabeling $M_b$ states to $b_1 = b_1''$ and $b_2 = b_2''$) getting $M_{b(b \cup \epsilon)b} = (\{b_1, b_2, b_1', b_2', b_1'', b_2'', s, \epsilon_1\}, \Sigma, \Delta_{b(b \cup \epsilon)b}, b_1', \{b_2''\})$ where $\Delta_{b(b \cup \epsilon)b} = \{((b_2', \epsilon), s), ((b_1, b), b_2), ((s, \epsilon), \epsilon_1), ((s, \epsilon), b_1), ((b_1', b), b_2'), ((b_1'', b), b_2''), ((b_2, \epsilon), b_1''), ((\epsilon_1, \epsilon), b_1'')\}$. Finally, we construct $(b(b \cup \epsilon)b)^*$ which takes the form $M = (\{b_1, b_2, b_1', b_2', b_1'', b_2'', s, \epsilon_1, s'\}, \Sigma, \Delta, s', \{s', b_2''\})$ where 
$$
\begin{align}
\Delta &= \{((b_2', \epsilon), s), ((b_1, b), b_2), ((s, \epsilon), \epsilon_1), ((s, \epsilon), b_1), ((b_1', b), b_2'), \\ &((b_1'', b), b_2''), ((b_2, \epsilon), b_1''), ((\epsilon_1, \epsilon), b_1''), ((s', \epsilon), b_1'), ((b_2'', \epsilon), s')\}
\end{align}
$$

b)
We begin with the primitive subexpressions $b$ and $a$. For $b$ we have $M_b = (\{b_1, b_2\}, \Sigma, \{((b_1, b), b_2)\}, b_1, \{b_2\})$ and for $a$ we have $M_a = (\{a_1, a_2\}, \Sigma, \{((a_1, a), a_2)\}, a_1, \{a_2\})$. Next we construct $ba$ giving $M_{ba} = (\{a_1, a_2, b_1, b_2\}, \Sigma, \{((a_1, a), a_2), ((b_1, b), b_2), ((b_2, \epsilon), a_1)\}, b_1, \{a_2\})$. Next, we construct $bab$ (after relabeling $M_b$ states to $b_1 = b_1'$ and $b_2 = b_2'$) getting $M_{bab} = (\{a_1, a_2, b_1, b_2, b_1', b_2'\}, \Sigma, \{((a_1, a), a_2), ((b_1, b), b_2), ((b_2, \epsilon), a_1), ((b_1', b), b_2'), ((a_2, \epsilon), b_1')\}, b_1, \{b_2'\})$. Now for the regular expression $a^*$ we construct $M_{a^*} = (\{s, a_1, a_2\}, \Sigma, \Delta_{a^*}, s, \{s, a_2\})$ where $\Delta_{a^*} = \{((a_1, a), a_2), ((s, \epsilon), a_1), ((a_2, \epsilon), s)\}$. Finally, we construct $bab \cup a^*$ (after relabeling $M_{a^*}$ states $a_1 = a_1'$ and $a_2 = a_2'$) giving $M = (\{s', s, a_1', a_2', a_1, a_2, b_1, b_2, b_1', b_2'\}, \Sigma, \Delta, s', \{s, a_2', b_2'\})$ where 
$$
\begin{align}
\Delta &= \{((a_1', a), a_2'), ((s, \epsilon), a_1'), ((a_2', \epsilon), s), ((a_1, a), a_2), ((b_1, b), b_2), ((b_2, \epsilon), a_1), \\ &((b_1', b), b_2'), ((a_2, \epsilon), b_1'), ((s', \epsilon), s), ((s', \epsilon), b_1)\}
\end{align}
$$

**Problem (1a, 1f, 1m, 1q, 1v)**
For each of the following languages L, state whether L is regular or not and prove your answer:
a) $\{a^ib^j | i, j \ge 0 \text{ and } i + j = 5\}$.
f) $\{w \in \{Y, N\}^* | \text{w contains at least two Y’s and at most two N’s}\}$.
m) 
$$
\left\{
w \in \{a,b\}^*
\;\middle|\;
\substack{
\text{the number of occurrences of the substring }ab\\
\text{equals the number of occurrences of the substring }ba
}
\right\}
$$

q) $\{w | w \in \{a - z\}^* \text{ every letter in w appears at least twice}\}$.
v) $\{w \in \{1\}^* | \text{w is, for some } n \ge 1\text{, the unary encoding of }10^n\}$. 
**Solution**
a)
$L$ is a regular language since $L = \{bbbbb, abbbb, aabbb, aaabb, aaaab, aaaaa\}$ which is finite and all finite languages are regular languages. To justify the equality, every string in L has the form $a^i b^j$ where $i + j = 5$. Thus the only possible pairs (i,j) are (0,5), (1,4), (2,3), (3,2), (4,1), and (5,0), giving exactly the six strings listed above. Conversely, each of those six strings has the form $a^i b^j$ with $i,j >= 0$ and $i + j = 5$. Hence the sets are equal.

f)
$L$ is a regular language. Define $L_1 = \{w \in \{Y, N\}^* | \text{w contains at least two Y’s}\}$ and $L_2 = \{w \in \{Y, N\}^* | \text{w contains at most two N’s}\}$. Then $L = L_1 \cap L_2$ and since intersections are closed under regular languages, it suffices to show that $L_1$ and $L_2$ are both regular. We do this with regular expressions. Notice $L_1 = L((Y \cup N)^*Y(Y \cup N)^*Y(Y \cup N)^*)$ and that $L_2 = L(Y^*NY^* \cup Y^*NY^*NY^* \cup Y^*)$. To justify the expressions, a string contains at least two Y's iff it can be written as xYyYz for some x,y,z in $\{Y,N\}^*$, which is exactly the language described by $(Y \cup N)^*Y(Y \cup N)^*Y(Y \cup N)^*$. Similarly, a string contains at most two N's iff it contains exactly zero, one, or two N's, corresponding to $Y^*NY^* \cup Y^*NY^*NY^* \cup Y^*$. Hence the stated regular expressions describe $L_1$ and $L_2$ exactly.

m)
$L$ is a regular language. $L = L(\epsilon \cup a \cup b \cup a(a\cup b)^*a \cup b(a \cup b)^*b)$. Since $L$ is definable by a regular expression, it must be a regular language. To justify the equality, observe that every occurrence of ab represents a transition from a to b, while every occurrence of ba represents a transition from b to a. If a nonempty string begins and ends with the same symbol, every transition from a to b is matched by a transition from b to a, so the numbers of ab and ba occurrences are equal. If the string begins with a and ends with b, there is exactly one more ab than ba, and if it begins with b and ends with a, there is exactly one more ba than ab. Therefore the counts are equal exactly when the string is epsilon, has length one, or begins and ends with the same symbol. 

q)
$L$ is a regular language. Define $L' = \bigcap_{i \in \{a-z\}}\{w | w \in \{a - z\}^* \text{ letter i in w does not appear once}\}$. We show that $L = L'$.  Consider $s \in L$ and some $K = \{w | w \in \{a - z\}^* \text{ letter } \lambda \text{ in w does not appear once}\}$ for arbitrary $\lambda \in \{a-z\}$. If $s = \epsilon$, then $s \in K$ since $\lambda$ is contained $0$ times in $s$. If $s \ne \epsilon$, then there is some letter $\alpha$ contained in $s$. $s \in K$ if $\lambda$ is not contained, else set $\alpha = \lambda$. By definition of $L$, letter $\alpha$ is contained at least twice in $s$. Hence $s \in K$ since $\alpha$ is not contained once. It follows that $s \in L'$ since $K$ was arbitrary, meaning $L \subseteq L'$. Now let $t \in L'$. If $t = \epsilon$, then $t \in L$ by definition so suppose not. Consider any letter $\beta \in \{a-z\}$ that $t$ contains. Because $t \in L'$ and $t$ contains at least one letter $\beta$, it must contain at least two $\beta$ else we contradict $t \in L'$. Hence $t \in L$ by construction. Therefore, $L = L'$. Finite intersection is closed under regular expressions, so to show that $L$ is a regular language, it suffices to show that arbitrary $Y = \{w | w \in \{a - z\}^* \text{ letter } \xi \text{ in w does not appear once}\}$ for $\xi \in \{a-z\}$ is a regular language. WLOG suppose that $\xi = a$. Define $A = a \cup b \cup c \cup ... \cup z$ and $A' = b \cup c \cup d \cup ... \cup z$ (union without $\xi$). Then $Y = L(A'^* \cup A^*aA^*aA^*)$ and so $Y$ is a regular language since it can be written as a regular expression. To justify the regular expression for Y, a string is in Y iff a does not occur exactly once. Thus a either occurs zero times or at least twice. If a occurs zero times, the string is generated by $A'^*$. If a occurs at least twice, the string can be written as xayaz for some x,y,z in $A^*$, and is therefore generated by $A^*aA^*aA^*$. Conversely, every string generated by $A'^*$ contains zero occurrences of a, while every string generated by $A^*aA^*aA^*$ contains at least two occurrences of a.

v)
For the sake of contradiction, suppose that $L$ is a regular language. Then there is some $k \ge 1$ that satisfies the pumping theorem. Let $w = 1^{10^k}$. Then $w$ is a unary encoding of $10^k$ and so $w \in L$. Also, $|w| = 10^k \ge k$. So we can write $w = xyz$ where $y \ne \epsilon$ and $|xy| \le k$ and $xy^iz \in L$ for every $i \ge 0$. Since $w$ consists of only $1$s we have $y = 1^p$ for some $1 \le p \le k$. Choose $i = 2$. Then $xy^2z = 1^{10^k + p}$. Hence $|xy^2z| = 10^k + p$. Since $1 \le p \le k$, we have $10^k < 10^k + p \le 10^k + k < 10^{k+1}$. Therefore, $xy^2z \not \in L$ since there is no power of $10$ between $10^k$ and $10^{k+1}$, a contradiction.

**Problem 15**
Let $L_1$ and $L_2$ be regular languages. Let $L$ be the language consisting of strings that are contained in exactly one of $L_1$ and $L_2$. Prove that $L$ is regular.
**Solution**
We show that $L = (L_1 \cup L_2) - (L_1 \cap L_2)$. Since union, intersection, and difference are all closed under regular languages, $L$ would also be a regular language. Consider some $s \in L$. By construction $s \in L_1$ or $s \in L_2$ but $s \not \in L_1 \cap L_2$. If $s \in L_1$, then $s \not \in L_2$ because   $s \not \in L_1 \cap L_2$. If $s \in L_2$, then $s \not \in L_1$ similarly. Hence, in either case, $s \in (L_1 \cup L_2) - (L_1 \cap L_2)$ meaning $L \subseteq (L_1 \cup L_2) - (L_1 \cap L_2)$. Now let $t \in (L_1 \cup L_2) - (L_1 \cap L_2)$. Then $t \in L_1$ or $t \in L_2$ and $t \not \in L_1 \cap L_2$. If $t \in L_1$, then since $t \not \in L_1 \cap L_2$, $t \not \in L_2$. Similarly, if $t \in L_2$ then $t \not \in L_1$. But then by definition of $L$, $t \in L$. Therefore, $L = (L_1 \cup L_2) - (L_1 \cap L_2)$. $\proofend$
