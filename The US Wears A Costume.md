## The Republic's Costume: Oligarchy in a 1776 Mask

The modern United States wears a costume tailored in 1776 - imperfect at that. When a citizen describes their home, the words "democracy" and "freedom" come to mind — the same vocabulary the Founders envisioned. The question worth sitting with is whether the American state still embodies those words, or merely performs them.

### The Monopoly of "Security"

A useful place to start is the recent FCC ban on foreign-made consumer routers — framed as a vital cybersecurity response to the Volt, Flax, and Salt Typhoon attacks on US infrastructure. The security concern is real. But the remedy is instructively strange.

The ban's primary target was TP-Link, which held roughly 65% of the US home router market. Cybersecurity experts told CNET that TP-Link's routers were exploited not because of unusual vulnerabilities, but because of their sheer ubiquity — the same class of vulnerabilities, as the US government acknowledged, were present in Cisco hardware. TP-Link, which had by this point relocated its global headquarters to California and voluntarily joined the federal Common Vulnerability and Exposures program, described Netgear's sustained public campaign against it as a "smear campaign" — and subsequently sued. Netgear's stock rose 16.7% on the day the ban was announced.

The structural oddity of the ban is where the lobbying fingerprints are clearest. The FCC's "Conditional Approval" pathway — the only route for any manufacturer to continue selling new models — does not primarily require demonstrating security. It requires companies to submit a plan for shifting manufacturing to the United States. A former FCC official told CyberScoop they were puzzled by the move and questioned whether it would survive legal challenge. One analyst described it as "an industrial policy requirement wrapped in security language." The exemption process, with no established timeline and no transparent criteria, is structurally open to political patronage. This is the digital evolution of **Project SHAMROCK**: the state does not need to manufacture a monopoly. It only needs to construct a gate, and then decide who gets the key.

### The Hostile State

The dynamics above might be dismissed as ordinary regulatory capture — lobbying as usual. The difficulty is that there exists a documented pattern of the state's relationship with its own citizens that goes considerably further.

In 1962, the Joint Chiefs of Staff submitted a proposal to Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara — signed by JCS Chairman Lyman Lemnitzer — outlining a series of false-flag operations against American citizens to justify a military invasion of Cuba. **Operation Northwoods** proposed staging bombings in US cities, hijacking civilian aircraft, and sinking boats of Cuban refugees, with the violence to be publicly attributed to Havana. The proposal was rejected by President Kennedy, who subsequently removed Lemnitzer as JCS Chairman. The documents remained classified for decades and only became public knowledge in 2001. The significance is not that the plan was executed. It is that it was formally proposed, signed, and submitted through the proper chain of command as a legitimate option for governance - with only one individual to stop it. It is worth noting that Northwoods only became public because it was rejected and then declassified forty years later. The declassification of a program is, almost by definition, evidence that the program ended or failed. What the archive cannot contain is what succeeded.

That hostility has modernized. Where the FBI once used **COINTELPRO** to dismantle civil rights movements through psychological warfare and blackmail — a program whose existence was only revealed through a 1971 burglary of an FBI field office — the state has since turned its intelligence apparatus toward its own digital infrastructure. The **Vault 7** leak revealed that the CIA had developed an extensive arsenal of tools capable of exploiting consumer devices, from smartphones to Samsung televisions. "US-approved" hardware carries no inherent guarantee of privacy from the state that approved it.

The treatment of those who make such programs public is instructive. Following the Vault 7 disclosures — which the CIA described internally as "the largest data loss in CIA history" — senior CIA officials under Director Mike Pompeo discussed kidnapping and, according to testimony from more than 30 former US officials reported by Yahoo News, requested "sketches" or "options" for the assassination of **WikiLeaks** founder Julian Assange, then sheltering in Ecuador's London embassy. The plans did not receive formal approval, in part because NSC lawyers questioned their legality and in part because, as officials noted, they would jeopardize the ongoing criminal case being built against him. The Justice Department subsequently expedited its indictment. Assange ultimately spent five years in Belmarsh Prison before pleading to a single count and being released in 2024. The former CIA officer who leaked the Vault 7 files was sentenced to forty years in prison. The state's message to those who reveal its tools is consistent, whatever legal mechanism it employs.

### The Erasure of Speculation

The state maintains its public image through a sophisticated relationship with the information environment. The CIA's covert relationships with journalists and media organizations were documented by the 1975 **Church Committee**, which confirmed the Agency maintained a network of foreign contacts to influence opinion through propaganda, and identified over fifty US journalists employed directly by the Agency — findings Carl Bernstein expanded in a landmark 1977 _Rolling Stone_ investigation. This was not an aberration; it was policy, documented in congressional testimony.

This creates a particular epistemological problem. The declassification of programs like **MKUltra** — a twenty-year program of illegal human experimentation and unconsensual drugging, funded through front organizations and conducted on citizens without their knowledge — has a double-edged effect. It proves that what was once dismissed as speculation was grounded in fact. But because these programs are exposed only decades after the fact, their immediate impact is neutralized. By the time the truth is admitted, the actors have changed and the political moment has passed. The pattern this establishes is worth noting: the time horizon for accountability is long enough that accountability rarely arrives.

The **Jeffrey Epstein** case illustrates what that looks like in practice. When federal prosecutors in the Southern District of Florida had assembled a draft 60-count federal indictment against Epstein in 2007 — with at least 30 identified victims — US Attorney Alex Acosta negotiated a Non-Prosecution Agreement that allowed Epstein to plead to minor state charges, serve 13 months in a minimum-security facility on work release, and — most remarkably — extended immunity not only to Epstein but to unnamed "potential co-conspirators." The agreement was filed under seal and, in violation of federal law, concealed from the victims themselves. The Justice Department's own Office of Professional Responsibility later concluded Acosta had exercised "poor judgment" — stopping short of finding professional misconduct — and Acosta went on to serve as Secretary of Labor before resigning under renewed scrutiny in 2019. What remains an open and legitimate question is how a 60-count federal indictment against a well-connected financier became a sealed agreement shielding unnamed co-conspirators.

### The Reality: A Managed Oligarchy

A 2014 Princeton study by **Gilens and Page** — published in _Perspectives on Politics_, a peer-reviewed political science journal — tested four theories of American governance against 1,779 policy outcomes over twenty years. Their finding: US policy correlates almost exclusively with the preferences of economic elites and organized interest groups, while the preferences of average citizens have a "near-zero, statistically non-significant" independent impact. The system reliably produces outcomes that serve concentrated interests, across administrations, across parties, across decades.

The costume of 1776 remains remarkably intact. The vocabulary of democracy, the rituals of elections, the language of freedom — all present. The question the evidence invites is not whether American democracy has been suddenly overthrown, but whether its mechanisms have been slowly hollowed out to the point where the performance and the substance have come apart. The Declaration of Independence listed twenty-seven grievances against the British Crown. It is left as an exercise for the reader to count how many have been exceeded.

What each citizen does with that count is, perhaps, the last genuinely free act the system cannot yet prevent.
