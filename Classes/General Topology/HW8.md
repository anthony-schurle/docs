1) Let $A \subset X$ and $B \subset Y$. If X and Y are connected, show that $(X \times Y) - (A \times B)$ is connected.
Suppose X and Y are connected. Since $(X \times Y) - (A \times B) = (X \times (Y - B)) \cup ((X - A) \times Y)$, by theorem 23.3 it suffices to show that $(X \times (Y - B))$ intersects $((X - A) \times Y)$ and both are connected. Since $B \subset Y$, $Y - B \ne \emptyset$. Then pick $y_0 \in Y - B$. Similarly, we choose $x_0 \in X - A$. But then we have $x_0 \in (X - A)$, $x_0 \in X$, $y_0 \in Y- B$, and $y_0 \in Y$ so that $x_0 \times y_0 \in (X \times (Y - B))$ and $x_0 \times y_0 \in ((X - A) \times Y)$. Therefore, $(X \times (Y - B))$ intersects $((X - A) \times Y)$. Now we show both are connected. Since $X \times \{y_0\}$ is homeomorphic to X, it is connected. Similarly, $\{x_0\} \times Y$ is connected. Both $X \times \{y_0\}$ and $\{x_0\} \times Y$ contain $x_0 \times y_0$, and so by theorem 23.3, $(X \times \{y_0\}) \cup (\{x_0\} \times Y) =: C$ is connected. Define $H_y := X \times \{y\}$ where $y \in Y - B$. Clearly all $H_y$ intersect $C$ at $x_0 \times y$ and each $H_y$ is homeomorphic to X, and so connected. Therefore, $\bigcup_{y \in Y - B}C \cup H_y = C \cup (X \times (Y - B))$ is connected by theorem 23.3. Define $H_x := \{x\} \times Y$ where $x \in X - A$. Clearly all $H_x$ intersect $C$ at $x \times y_0$ and each $H_x$ is homeomorphic to Y, and so connected. Therefore, $\bigcup_{x \in X - A}C \cup H_x = C \cup ((X-A) \times Y$ is connected by theorem 23.3. So, by theorem 23.3 again, $(C \cup ((X-A) \times Y) \cup (C \cup (X \times (Y - B))) = C \cup (X \times (Y - B)) \cup ((X - A) \times Y)$ is connected. But $C \subseteq (X \times (Y - B)) \cup ((X - A) \times Y)$ and so $C \cup (X \times (Y - B)) \cup ((X - A) \times Y) = (X \times (Y - B)) \cup ((X - A) \times Y)$. Thus, $(X \times Y) - (A \times B)$ is connected. $\blacksquare$

2) Let $\{X_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in J}$ be an indexed family of connected spaces; let X be the product space $X = \prod_{\alpha \in J}X_\alpha$. Let $a = \{a_\alpha\}$ be a fixed point of X.
a) Given any finite subset K of J, let $X_K$ denote the subspace of X consisting of all points $x = (x_\alpha)$ such that $x_\alpha = a_\alpha$ for $\alpha \not \in K$. Show that $X_K$ is connected.

 $X_K$ is homeomorphic to $\prod_{\alpha \in K} X_\alpha$, a finite cartesian product of connected spaces; connected by theorem 23.6.
 
 b) Show that the union Y of the spaces $X_K$ is connected.
 By definition of $X_K$, each $X_K$ contains point a. It follows that $X_K \cap X_L \ne \emptyset$ for any K and L. And since each $X_K$ is a connected subspace of X, by theorem 23.3, their union (Y) is connected. 

c) Show that X equals the closure of Y; conclude that X is connected.
Suppose for arbitrary $z = \{z_\alpha\}$ is a point contained in X. By theorem 17.5, to show that $z \in \overline{Y}$, it suffices to show that every basis element containing z intersects Y. Let B be an arbitrary basis element of X containing z. By theorem 19.1, B is $\prod b_\alpha$ where $b_\alpha$ is open in $X_\alpha$ and $b_\alpha = X_\alpha$ except for finitely many $\alpha$. Define $I$ to be all such $\alpha$ where $b_\alpha \ne X_\alpha$. Define the coordinates of w as such: for $\alpha \in I$, let $w_\alpha = z_\alpha$ and for $\alpha \not \in I$, let $w_\alpha = a_\alpha$. We see that $w \in B$ since each $w_\alpha \in b_\alpha$ for all $\alpha$. By definition, $w \in X_I$ and since $X_I \subseteq Y$, it follows that $w \in Y$. Because $w \in Y$ and $w \in B$, we have $w \in B \cap Y$. But B was arbitrary, and so every basis element containing z intersects Y. Therefore, $z \in \overline{Y}$. Therefore, because z was arbitrary,  $X \subseteq \overline{Y}$.  $\overline{Y} \subseteq X$ trivially since $Y \subseteq X$. Therefore, $\overline{Y} = X$. Now we have that $Y \subseteq X \subseteq \overline{Y}$ so that X is connected by theorem 23.4. $\blacksquare$

3) Let $p: X \to Y$ be a quotient map. Show that if each set $p^{-1}(\{y\})$ is connected, and if Y is connected, then X is connected.
Suppose each set $p^{-1}(\{y\})$ is connected and Y is connected. For contradiction, suppose X was disconnected by separation $U, V$. We show that U and V are saturated. Suppose not. Then there is some $u \in Y$ where $p^{-1}(\{u\})$ intersects U and V. Since U and V separate X, the sets $U \cap p^{-1}(\{u\})$ and $V \cap p^{-1}(\{u\})$ would form a separation of $p^{-1}(\{u\})$, contradicting its connectedness. Therefore, U and V are saturated sets. It follows that $p(U)$ and $p(V)$ are open in Y. We show that these sets are disjoint and cover Y. Suppose $y \in p(U) \cap p(V)$. Then $p^{-1}(\{y\})$ is intersected by both U and V. Since U and V are disjoint, this forces a partition of $p^{-1}(\{y\})$ contradicting its connectedness. Therefore, $p(V)$ and $p(U)$ are disjoint. Trivially, $p(U) \cup p(V) \subseteq Y$ by definition of p. Since p is a surjection, also trivially $Y \subseteq p(X) = p(U \cup V) = p(U) \cup p(V)$. Therefore, $Y = p(U) \cup p(V)$. Since $p(U)$ and $p(V)$ are disjoint sets covering Y, they form a separation which is a contradiction. Thus, X is connected. $\blacksquare$

4) Let $f: S^1 \to \mathbb{R}$ be a continuous map. Show that there exists a point x of $S^1$ such that $f(x) = f(-x)$.

Define a function $g: S^1 \to \mathbb{R}$ by $g(x) = f(x) - f(-x)$. Since $f(x)$ is continuous, the composition $f(-x)$ is continuous, and thus their difference $g(x)$ is continuous. Pick an arbitrary point $x_0 \in S^1$. If $g(x_0) = 0$, then $f(x_0) = f(-x_0)$ and we are done. Suppose $g(x_0) \ne 0$. WLOG, assume $g(x_0) > 0$. We have $g(-x_0) = f(-x_0) - f(-(-x_0)) = f(-x_0) - f(x_0) = -(f(x_0) - f(-x_0)) = -g(x_0)$. Since $g(x_0) > 0$, it follows that $g(-x_0) < 0$. Because $S^1$ is a connected space and $g$ is continuous, the image $g(S^1)$ is a connected subspace of $\mathbb{R}$ by theorem 23.5. The connected subspaces of $\mathbb{R}$ are intervals. Since $g(S^1)$ contains both a positive value $g(x_0)$ and a negative value $g(-x_0)$, by theorem 24.3, $g(S^1)$ must contain 0. Therefore, there exists some $c \in S^1$ such that $g(c) = 0$. By definition of $g$, $f(c) - f(-c) = 0$, and thus $f(c) = f(-c)$. $\blacksquare$

6) Let $f: X \to X$ be continuous. Show that if $X = [0, 1]$, there is a point x such that $f(x) = x$. The point x is called a fixed point of f. What happens if X equals $[0, 1)$ or $(0, 1)$?

Define a function $g: [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ by $g(x) = f(x) - x$. Since $f$ is continuous and the identity function $x$ is continuous, their difference $g(x)$ is continuous. We evaluate the endpoints. For $x = 0$, $g(0) = f(0) - 0 = f(0)$. Since the codomain of $f$ is $[0, 1]$, $f(0) \ge 0$, so $g(0) \ge 0$. For $x = 1$, $g(1) = f(1) - 1$. Since the codomain is $[0, 1]$, $f(1) \le 1$, so $g(1) \le 0$. If $g(0) = 0$ or $g(1) = 0$, then $x=0$ or $x=1$ is a fixed point and we are done. Suppose neither is a fixed point. Then we must have $g(0) > 0$ and $g(1) < 0$. Because $[0, 1]$ is connected and $g$ is continuous, $g([0, 1])$ is a connected interval in $\mathbb{R}$. Since $0$ lies strictly between $g(1)$ and $g(0)$, by the Intermediate Value Theorem, there exists some $c \in (0, 1)$ such that $g(c) = 0$. By definition of $g$, $f(c) - c = 0$, so $f(c) = c$. Thus, $c$ is a fixed point.

If $X = [0, 1)$: The property fails. Let $f(x) = \frac{x + 1}{2}$. The image is $[0.5, 1)$, which is contained in $[0, 1)$. Setting $f(x) = x$ yields $\frac{x + 1}{2} = x \implies x = 1$. Since $1 \notin [0, 1)$, there is no fixed point.
If $X = (0, 1)$: The property fails. Let $f(x) = \frac{x}{2}$. The image is $(0, 0.5) \subset (0, 1)$. Setting $f(x) = x$ yields $x = 0$. Since $0 \notin (0, 1)$, there is no fixed point. $\blacksquare$
